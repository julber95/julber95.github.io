I study artificial intelligence at Université PSL, in the IASD master's programme, and work as an AI
engineer on a work-study contract. Before that I did a double bachelor's in AI and
economics at Paris-Dauphine, which left me with two habits: asking what a number actually measures, and
distrusting any result that arrives without its baseline.

What draws me to machine learning is usually the part *after* the training loop. A GAN's discriminator still
knows things the generator never learned. A language model's text depends as much on how you read from it as
on what it learned. A classifier's prediction is only half an answer until you can say which words produced it,
and whether that explanation survives when you delete those words. The plates in this atlas are mostly about
that second half: sampling, decoding, explaining, and the question of whether a model has learned the
thing or only a proxy for it.

I also tend to prefer small models that can be read over large ones that can only be trusted. That isn't a
position against scale. A model you can take apart simply teaches you more.

## The design side

On the side, I've done visual-identity and graphic-design work as a volunteer: for ALIAS (2024–2025), and
earlier through France Bénévolat (2020). That's where this atlas comes from. I wanted
a site that looked made by hand, so I drew one, in code: the typography, the plates, the field of points on the index.
